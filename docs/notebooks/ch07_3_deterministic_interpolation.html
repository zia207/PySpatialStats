1. Overview¶

Four interpolators, four very different assumptions about how a field behaves between observations — from nearest-neighbour's blunt "copy the closest value" to the thin-plate spline's globally smooth bending surface. None of them models spatial correlation explicitly (Chapter 7.1, Section 6); all four are purely geometric rules for turning point measurements into a predicted surface, and all four share one interface so they can be swapped freely.

Method Idea Exact? Extrapolates smoothly?
NearestNeighbor Copy the closest observation (Voronoi tessellation) Yes No — sharp boundaries
TIN Piecewise-linear over a Delaunay triangulation Yes No — flat outside the convex hull
IDW Weighted average, weight $\propto 1/d^p$ Yes (at $p<\infty$, no smoothing) Yes, but flattens far from data
RBF (thin-plate) The smoothest possible surface interpolating the data Yes (smoothing=0) Can overshoot far from data

3. One Interface for Every Method¶

Every interpolator in this Part — deterministic here, geostatistical in Chapter 7.4 — shares the same four methods: .fit(coords, values), .predict(new_coords), .predict_grid(region, ...), and .clone() / .get_params() (the same scikit-learn-style pattern Part 6's GW models used). This is what lets Chapter 7.5's cross_validate and compare_methods treat every method identically, without any method-specific code.

In [2]:
demo = IDW(power=2, k=12)
print("get_params():", demo.get_params())
demo2 = demo.clone()
demo2.power = 3
print("clone is independent:", demo.power, "vs", demo2.power)

4. Nearest Neighbour (Voronoi)¶

The simplest possible interpolator: every prediction location copies its closest observation's value exactly. The implicit spatial partition is a Voronoi tessellation — one polygon per monitor, containing every point closer to that monitor than to any other.

In [3]:
nn = NearestNeighbor()
nn.fit(coords, values)
grid_nn, pred_nn = nn.predict_grid(coords, n_cells=100)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 7))
im = ax.imshow(grid_nn.to_raster(pred_nn), extent=grid_nn.extent, origin="lower", cmap="viridis")
ax.scatter(coords[:, 0], coords[:, 1], s=15, color="white", edgecolor="k", linewidth=0.4)
plt.colorbar(im, ax=ax, label="PM2.5 (ug/m3)", fraction=0.046)
ax.set_title("Nearest-neighbour surface (Voronoi tessellation)")
ax.set_aspect("equal")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig("nearest_surface.png", dpi=100, bbox_inches="tight")
plt.show()

The sharp polygon boundaries are the Voronoi cell edges made visible — exactly where the nearest monitor switches from one to another. This is a useful sanity check and a fast fallback, but as an interpolator it throws away every piece of information except "which monitor is closest," ignoring every other nearby reading entirely.

5. TIN (Delaunay Linear)¶

TIN builds a Delaunay triangulation over the monitors and interpolates linearly within each triangle — the piecewise-linear analogue of natural-neighbour interpolation (true Sibson natural-neighbour interpolation is not implemented in this library). Outside the convex hull of the monitor network, no triangle exists, so TIN falls back to its fill strategy ('nearest' by default).

In [4]:
tin = TIN(fill="nearest")
tin.fit(coords, values)
grid_tin, pred_tin = tin.predict_grid(coords, n_cells=100)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 7))
im = ax.imshow(grid_tin.to_raster(pred_tin), extent=grid_tin.extent, origin="lower", cmap="viridis")
ax.scatter(coords[:, 0], coords[:, 1], s=15, color="white", edgecolor="k", linewidth=0.4)
plt.colorbar(im, ax=ax, label="PM2.5 (ug/m3)", fraction=0.046)
ax.set_title("TIN (Delaunay linear) surface")
ax.set_aspect("equal")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig("tin_surface.png", dpi=100, bbox_inches="tight")
plt.show()

Within the triangulated interior, TIN produces a much smoother-looking surface than nearest-neighbour — no sharp cell boundaries, just continuous linear gradients between monitors. The boundary between the triangulated interior and the fill="nearest" exterior is visible as a subtle change in texture near the domain edges, worth remembering when interpreting predictions far from any monitor.

6. Inverse Distance Weighting¶

$$ \hat{z}(\mathbf{s}_0) = \frac{\sum_i w_i\, z_i}{\sum_i w_i}, \qquad w_i = \frac{1}{d_i^{\,p}} $$

power controls how sharply influence falls off with distance; k limits the weighted average to the $k$ nearest neighbours (all monitors contribute if k=None); radius instead caps the neighbourhood by distance.

In [5]:
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(15, 5.5))
for ax, power in zip(axes, (1, 2, 4)):
    idw = IDW(power=power, k=12)
    idw.fit(coords, values)
    grid_idw, pred_idw = idw.predict_grid(coords, n_cells=100)
    im = ax.imshow(grid_idw.to_raster(pred_idw), extent=grid_idw.extent, origin="lower", cmap="viridis",
                   vmin=values.min(), vmax=values.max())
    ax.scatter(coords[:, 0], coords[:, 1], s=8, color="white", edgecolor="k", linewidth=0.3)
    ax.set_title(f"IDW, power={power}")
    ax.set_aspect("equal")
fig.colorbar(im, ax=axes, label="PM2.5 (ug/m3)", fraction=0.025, pad=0.02)
plt.savefig("idw_power_comparison.png", dpi=100, bbox_inches="tight")
plt.show()

Higher power sharpens the local "bullseye" pattern around each monitor — at power=4, the surface looks almost like a smoothed version of nearest-neighbour's Voronoi cells, since distant monitors contribute negligible weight. Lower power produces a much smoother, more globally averaged surface. Chapter 7.5 tunes this trade-off by cross-validated RMSE rather than by eye.

7. RBF / Thin-Plate Spline¶

The thin-plate spline is, informally, the smoothest possible surface that still passes through every observation — imagine bending a thin metal sheet to touch every data point while minimizing total curvature. smoothing > 0 relaxes the exact-interpolation requirement (Chapter 7.1, Section 5), useful when the observations themselves are noisy.

In [6]:
rbf = RBF(kernel="thin_plate_spline", smoothing=0.0)
rbf.fit(coords, values)
grid_rbf, pred_rbf = rbf.predict_grid(coords, n_cells=100)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 7))
im = ax.imshow(grid_rbf.to_raster(pred_rbf), extent=grid_rbf.extent, origin="lower", cmap="viridis",
               vmin=values.min(), vmax=values.max())
ax.scatter(coords[:, 0], coords[:, 1], s=15, color="white", edgecolor="k", linewidth=0.4)
plt.colorbar(im, ax=ax, label="PM2.5 (ug/m3)", fraction=0.046)
ax.set_title("Thin-plate spline surface")
ax.set_aspect("equal")
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig("rbf_surface.png", dpi=100, bbox_inches="tight")
plt.show()

print(f"prediction range: {pred_rbf.min():.1f} to {pred_rbf.max():.1f} (observed range: {values.min():.1f} to {values.max():.1f})")

prediction range: -1.7 to 18.0 (observed range: 2.1 to 14.4)

Notice the predicted range printed above: the thin-plate spline's global smoothness constraint lets it overshoot past the observed data range, especially in sparsely sampled regions (visible as unusually bright or dark patches far from any monitor) — and here it overshoots badly enough to predict a physically impossible negative PM2.5 concentration in at least one sparse corner of the domain, despite every observed value being strictly positive. This is a real cost of the "smoothest possible surface" property that IDW and TIN, being local weighted averages bounded by their neighbours' values, structurally cannot produce — a concrete reason to clip or sanity-check spline output before treating it as a usable prediction.

8. Comparing the Four Surfaces¶

In [7]:
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 4, figsize=(19, 5.5))
methods = {"Nearest": (grid_nn, pred_nn), "TIN": (grid_tin, pred_tin),
          "IDW (p=2)": (make_grid(coords, n_cells=100), None), "Thin-plate spline": (grid_rbf, pred_rbf)}
idw2 = IDW(power=2, k=12); idw2.fit(coords, values)
g_idw2, p_idw2 = idw2.predict_grid(coords, n_cells=100)
methods["IDW (p=2)"] = (g_idw2, p_idw2)

for ax, (name, (g, p)) in zip(axes, methods.items()):
    im = ax.imshow(g.to_raster(p), extent=g.extent, origin="lower", cmap="viridis",
                   vmin=values.min(), vmax=values.max())
    ax.scatter(coords[:, 0], coords[:, 1], s=6, color="white", edgecolor="k", linewidth=0.2)
    ax.set_title(name)
    ax.set_aspect("equal")
    ax.set_xticks([]); ax.set_yticks([])
fig.colorbar(im, ax=axes, label="PM2.5 (ug/m3)", fraction=0.02, pad=0.02)
plt.savefig("four_methods_comparison.png", dpi=100, bbox_inches="tight")
plt.show()

Side by side, the trade-off is visible directly: nearest-neighbour's sharp cells, TIN's angular facets, IDW's smooth "bullseyes," and the spline's very smooth but occasionally overshooting surface. None of these panels carries a confidence statement — that is exactly what Chapter 7.4's kriging adds. Chapter 7.5 replaces "which looks smoothest" with an honest, cross-validated answer to "which actually predicts best."

9. Summary and Conclusions¶

This chapter fit all four deterministic interpolators on the same 159-monitor network, through one shared interface.

What we did¶

  1. Confirmed every interpolator in this Part shares .fit/.predict/.predict_grid/.clone/.get_params.
  2. Mapped nearest-neighbour's Voronoi tessellation, TIN's piecewise-linear surface, IDW at three power values, and the thin-plate spline — on the identical 159-monitor network and prediction grid.
  3. Found the thin-plate spline predicts a physically impossible negative PM2.5 value in a sparsely sampled corner of the domain, despite every observed value being strictly positive — a direct, visible consequence of its global-smoothness construction that the local, weighted-average methods (IDW, TIN) structurally cannot produce.
  4. Compared all four surfaces side by side on a shared color scale.

Practical takeaways¶

  • Higher IDW power values behave more like nearest-neighbour; lower values behave more like a broad smoothing average — treat power as a tunable hyperparameter (Chapter 7.5), not a fixed convention.
  • Watch for spline overshoot in sparse regions before trusting a thin-plate spline surface at face value.
  • None of these four methods produces a prediction variance — every map here is a single best guess, with no built-in way to say how much to trust any particular pixel.

Next steps¶
