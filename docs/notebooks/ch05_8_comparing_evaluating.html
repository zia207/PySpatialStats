1. Overview¶

Every method built in Chapters 5.5–5.6 produces a smoothed estimate. This chapter asks the question that actually matters: is it better, and by how much, and does it introduce new problems of its own? Because Chapter 5.3 built a simulation with a known true risk surface, this is not a matter of opinion — it can be scored directly.

Step What we do
Visual Maps with identical class breaks, a raw-vs-smoothed scatter, and the funnel plot with smoothed values overlaid
Formal scoring evaluate_smoothing — RMSE, bias, rank agreement, and credible-interval coverage against the known truth
Autocorrelation Smoothing mechanically raises Moran's I — with one instructive exception, checked directly
Hot spots Do raw and smoothed data agree on where the Gi* hot spots are, and does smoothing help or hurt precision?
Over-smoothing What happens to a genuine, sharp local excess under too strong a spatial prior

3. Visual Comparison: Maps with Identical Breaks¶

Fit every method once on this chapter's single simulated dataset, and map them with identical class breaks — without this, a subtler pattern in a smoothed map can look artificially different just because the colour scale shifted.

The raw SMR map is visibly noisier — speckled extreme values that do not appear in the truth panel; BYM's map is the closest visual match to the truth, EB (global) sits in between (smoother than raw, but not respecting the spatial pattern the way BYM does).

5. Formal Evaluation Against a Known Truth¶

The single-replicate maps above are suggestive; a defensible comparison needs to average over simulation noise. Six independent replicates (fresh true risk surface and fresh simulated counts each time) give a stable estimate of each method's real performance.

In [4]:
t0 = time.time()
all_results = []
for rep in range(6):
    theta_r = simulate_relative_risk(w, sigma=0.35, spatial_strength=0.95, seed=100 + rep)
    O_r = simulate_counts(theta_r, E_sim, seed=200 + rep)
    raw_r = smr(O_r, E_sim)
    eb_r = eb_gamma_poisson(O_r, E_sim, method="mle")
    loc_r = eb_local(O_r, E_sim, w)
    bym_r = BYM(O_r, E_sim, w, model="bym", backend="gibbs", draws=2000, tune=2000, chains=4, thin=10,
               seed=300 + rep)
    ev = evaluate_smoothing(theta_r, {"Raw SMR": raw_r, "EB (global)": eb_r.estimate,
                                      "Local EB": loc_r.estimate, "BYM": bym_r.rr_mean},
                            expected=E_sim, intervals={"BYM": (bym_r.rr_lo, bym_r.rr_hi)})
    all_results.append(ev)
print(f"6 replicates: {time.time() - t0:.0f}s")

mean_scores = pd.concat(all_results, keys=range(6)).groupby(level=1).mean()
mean_scores.round(4)

Averaged over six independent simulated datasets: BYM has the lowest RMSE (0.114) and highest rank agreement (Spearman 0.940), with Local EB close behind (RMSE 0.119, Spearman 0.936); global EB improves modestly over the raw SMR (RMSE 0.148 vs. 0.161, Spearman unchanged at 0.899 — rank order is invariant to the affine rescaling EB applies here) but falls well short of the spatial methods, because it only borrows strength from the whole region rather than genuinely nearby counties. BYM's 95% credible intervals cover the true value about 96% of the time — close to nominal — with a mean width of 0.48 on the relative-risk scale.

6. Spatial Structure: Smoothing Raises Autocorrelation by Construction¶

Do not read a smoothed map's higher Moran's I as evidence of a real spatial process — spatial smoothers are, by construction, built to make neighbours resemble each other more, so their output is autocorrelated almost by definition, independent of whether the underlying truth has any spatial structure at all.

In [5]:
for label, est in [("Raw SMR", raw), ("EB (global)", eb.estimate), ("Local EB", loc.estimate), ("BYM", bym.rr_mean)]:
    m = Moran(est, w_row, permutations=0)
    print(f"{label:14s} Moran's I = {m.I:.4f}")

Raw SMR        Moran's I = 0.6995
EB (global)    Moran's I = 0.6995
Local EB       Moran's I = 0.8020
BYM            Moran's I = 0.8036

Local EB and BYM (both explicitly spatial) show a clear jump in Moran's I (0.70 → 0.80) relative to the raw SMR — exactly the mechanical effect to be cautious about. Global EB's Moran's I is identical to the raw SMR's, which looks surprising until the reason is seen directly: because every county here has the same expected count ($E=40$ throughout this simulation), gamma–Poisson EB applies the exact same shrinkage weight to every county — a single common affine rescaling of the raw SMR, which leaves Moran's I (a correlation-based statistic, invariant to positive affine transforms) completely unchanged. With unequal $E_i$ (as in the real data, Chapters 5.4–5.5), global EB's weights would vary by county and this exact equality would not hold — this simulation's constant-$E$ design happens to isolate the point cleanly.

7. Hot Spots: Raw vs. Smoothed¶

Does smoothing change which counties register as Gi* hot spots (Part 4), and does it improve precision against the known truth?

In [6]:
gi_raw = getis_ord_gi(raw, w_row, star=True, inference="analytic", correction="fdr")
gi_bym = getis_ord_gi(bym.rr_mean, w_row, star=True, inference="analytic", correction="fdr")

true_high = theta_true > np.quantile(theta_true, 0.75)   # top quartile of the KNOWN true risk
print(f"raw SMR   Gi* hot spots: {int(gi_raw.hot.sum())}  "
      f"(all in top true-risk quartile: {int((gi_raw.hot & true_high).sum())})")
print(f"BYM       Gi* hot spots: {int(gi_bym.hot.sum())}  "
      f"(all in top true-risk quartile: {int((gi_bym.hot & true_high).sum())})")
print(f"true positives found by raw only : {int((gi_raw.hot & ~gi_bym.hot).sum())}")
print(f"true positives found by BYM only : {int((gi_bym.hot & ~gi_raw.hot).sum())}")

raw SMR   Gi* hot spots: 22  (all in top true-risk quartile: 22)
BYM       Gi* hot spots: 25  (all in top true-risk quartile: 25)
true positives found by raw only : 0
true positives found by BYM only : 3

At this simulation's signal strength ($\sigma=0.35$, $E=40$), both raw SMR and BYM-smoothed Gi* hot spots are 100% precise against the top true-risk quartile in this replicate — no false hot spots from either, because the underlying spatial signal here is strong enough that raw noise did not happen to create a spurious cluster. BYM's hot-spot set is a strict superset of the raw set (all of raw's hits, plus additional true positives) — smoothing recovered real signal that raw SMR's noise-inflated intervals had obscured, without introducing new false positives in this case. This is not a guarantee: at a weaker signal or smaller $E$, raw SMR's Gi* would be expected to produce real false positives that smoothing removes — Chapter 5.11, Exercise 1 asks for exactly that demonstration at a genuinely rare-outcome scale.

8. Over-smoothing and Prior Sensitivity¶

A spatial prior that is too strong can erase a genuine, sharp local excess by treating it as noise to be smoothed away. priors={'tau_u': (a, b)} lets the ICAR precision's prior be tightened directly.

In [7]:
bym_default = BYM(O_sim, E_sim, w, model="bym", backend="gibbs", draws=2000, tune=2000, chains=4, thin=10,
                  seed=1, priors={"tau_u": (1.0, 0.01)})
bym_strong = BYM(O_sim, E_sim, w, model="bym", backend="gibbs", draws=2000, tune=2000, chains=4, thin=10,
                 seed=1, priors={"tau_u": (10.0, 0.01)})   # a-priori favours LARGE tau_u = LESS spatial variation

sharpest = np.argmax(np.abs(np.log(theta_true)))
print(f"county with the most extreme true log-risk: {counties.iloc[sharpest]['COUNTY']}, "
      f"true theta={theta_true[sharpest]:.3f}")
print(f"default prior : BYM estimate = {bym_default.rr_mean[sharpest]:.3f}")
print(f"strong tau_u prior (less spatial variation allowed): BYM estimate = {bym_strong.rr_mean[sharpest]:.3f}")
print(f"raw SMR at that county: {raw[sharpest]:.3f}")

county with the most extreme true log-risk: Erie County, true theta=2.784
default prior : BYM estimate = 2.410
strong tau_u prior (less spatial variation allowed): BYM estimate = 2.396
raw SMR at that county: 2.475

Tightening the prior toward larger $\tau_u$ (which favours less spatial variation) pulls the sharpest true local excess (Erie County, true $\theta=2.78$) a little further from both its true value and the raw SMR: from 2.410 under the default prior to 2.396 under the tightened one, both below the raw SMR of 2.475. The shift is modest here — 217 counties' worth of real data already anchor $\tau_u$ fairly firmly even under a tighter prior — but the direction is exactly what over-smoothing predicts, and a still-tighter prior, or a sparser dataset with less data to overrule it, would make the effect more pronounced. The default prior (Gamma(1, 0.01), weakly informative) is deliberately diffuse to let the data determine how much spatial smoothing is warranted rather than imposing a strong assumption; tightening it without a principled reason trades away exactly the kind of genuine local signal disease mapping exists to find, even when the trade is small.

9. Summary and Conclusions¶

This chapter formally validated every smoothing method built in Chapters 5.5–5.6 against a known truth.

What we did¶

  1. Mapped truth, raw SMR, global EB, and BYM with identical class breaks — BYM visually closest to the truth, raw SMR visibly noisier.
  2. Built raw-vs-smoothed and funnel-style comparison plots showing smoothing's narrowed spread directly.
  3. Ran the formal 6-replicate evaluation: BYM (RMSE 0.114, Spearman 0.940) and Local EB (0.119, 0.936) best, global EB a modest improvement over raw SMR (0.148 vs. 0.161), BYM's 95% intervals covering the truth ≈96% of the time.
  4. Confirmed spatial smoothers mechanically raise Moran's I (0.70→0.80) — and found the one case where they do not (global EB under equal expected counts, an affine-invariance argument, not a general result).
  5. Compared Gi* hot spots on raw vs. smoothed data against the known true high-risk counties: both were 100% precise in this replicate, with BYM recovering additional true positives raw SMR's noise obscured.
  6. Demonstrated over-smoothing directly: a tightened tau_u prior pulled the sharpest true local excess further from both its true value and the raw data.

Practical takeaways¶

  • Trust the formal, replicated evaluation (Section 5) over any single-map impression — visual comparisons are suggestive, not conclusive, on their own.
  • Never interpret a smoothed map's higher spatial autocorrelation as new evidence of a real spatial process — it is largely mechanical, and Section 6 shows the one clean exception is itself simulation-specific, not a general escape from the caveat.
  • Check prior sensitivity explicitly (Section 8) before reporting a smoothed estimate — a well-intentioned but too-strong spatial prior can erase real signal, not just noise.
