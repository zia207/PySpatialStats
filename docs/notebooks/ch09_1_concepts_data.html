1. Overview¶

Every earlier Part treated space and (implicitly) a single point in time together. This Part adds time as a genuine second dimension of structure — not just "the same analysis, repeated at different dates," but the question of whether patterns in time are themselves spatially patterned, and vice versa.

This is not a gap this Part papers over. Every chapter that follows says explicitly, up front, which of its content is:

  • Directly supported by an existing module (esda.Moran/Moran_Diff applied year by year, cluster.getis_ord_gi per year, gwmodel.GTWR) — genuine library functionality, just applied across a time dimension rather than once.
  • Built by hand in the notebook, from smaller pieces this library already provides (a Knox test, Mann-Kendall trend categorization, two-way fixed-effects panel regression) — real, working code, but not a single spatialstats function call.
  • Not attempted, where the honest answer is that a faithful version needs infrastructure (a true space-time variogram, a Kronecker space-time weights matrix, non-separable ST-KDE) this repository does not yet have, and where hand-building a convincing approximation risks teaching something subtly wrong.

4. The Space-Time Cube¶

Data indexed by both location and time naturally form a cube: one axis for space (itself often two-dimensional), one axis for time. Part 2's tools (Moran's I, LISA) were all built for a single 2-D slice of this cube — a snapshot. The central methodological question of this whole Part is: what changes when the cube has a third axis, and when is "just slice it and reuse Part 2's tools" actually a safe simplification?

5. Separable vs. Non-Separable Structure¶

A space-time covariance (or, informally, a space-time pattern) is separable if it factors into a purely spatial component times a purely temporal component: $$ C(\mathbf{s}, t) = C_s(\mathbf{s}) \cdot C_t(t) $$ Separability is a strong, convenient assumption — it means the spatial pattern of correlation looks the same at every point in time, just rescaled by a time-varying factor, and a great deal of space-time machinery (including much of what spatialstats.spacetime's stub docstring describes) is built to exploit it when it holds. It is also frequently wrong in practice: a disease outbreak that starts in one region and spreads outward has a spatial correlation structure that genuinely changes shape over time (an expanding cluster), not just in magnitude — exactly the kind of pattern a separable model cannot represent no matter how its parameters are tuned. Chapter 9.3's per-year Gi* categorization (new, intensifying, persistent, diminishing, sporadic, oscillating hot spots) is, in effect, a hand-built way of detecting non-separable behaviour: a hot spot whose spatial footprint genuinely moves or changes shape over the 15 years is showing exactly the kind of time-varying spatial structure a separable model would smooth away.

7. The Crime Point Dataset: a CRS Gotcha, Verified¶

crime_data_2018.csv carries two coordinate pairs: long/lat and x_m/y_m. The outline warns the projected pair sits in a "mis-zoned CRS" — worth confirming directly, the same way earlier Parts always verified rather than assumed a provenance warning.

print(f"rows: {len(crime):,}   incident types: {crime['incident_type'].nunique()}")

Confirmed: x_m/y_m do not match any sensible projection of the same long/lat values — correctly projecting to UTM zone 17N (the right zone for this longitude) gives coordinates on a completely different scale than the file's own x_m/y_m column. Whatever CRS produced x_m/y_m, it is not consistent with the long/lat pair in the same file. This chapter, and every later one in this Part that uses point locations, projects fresh from long/lat rather than trusting x_m/y_m — exactly the outline's own guidance, verified rather than taken on faith.

8. Summary and Conclusions¶

This chapter set the conceptual stage for the whole Part, and established both datasets on solid ground before any real analysis begins.

What we did¶

  1. Confirmed directly that spatialstats.spacetime is an empty stub — zero implemented functions — and laid out how each following chapter will be explicit about what is library-supported, hand-built, or not attempted.
  2. Introduced the space-time "cube" and confirmed the county panel is genuinely balanced: 3,107 counties x 15 years, no gaps.
  3. Found the file's own name (..._2010_...) understates its actual span (1998–2012, 15 years) — a small, checkable provenance mismatch.
  4. Explained separability and why real space-time processes (an expanding disease cluster, a moving hot spot) often are not separable — directly motivating Chapter 9.3's per-year hot-spot categorization.
  5. Confirmed the crime dataset's x_m/y_m columns do not match any sensible projection of its own long/lat — verified numerically, not just taken on the outline's word — and established long/lat, freshly projected to UTM 17N, as the trustworthy coordinate source for the rest of this Part.

Practical takeaways¶

  • Before trusting a spatialstats.spacetime-shaped claim anywhere in this Part, check Section 3's list: is this a real library call, a hand-built demonstration, or explicitly out of scope?
  • A balanced panel (every unit observed every period, no gaps) is a genuine convenience — always check before assuming it, the way Section 6 did here.
  • When a dataset ships two coordinate systems, verify they actually agree before picking one — Section 7's check took two lines of code and caught a real, silent inconsistency.
